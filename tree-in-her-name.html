01

The thesis

The reason to make this film is that Cynthia Muge's strongest asset currently exists only as a press release about things she gave away. A documentary is the instrument that turns distribution into a record.

Every published number attached to Kahawa na Mama counts inputs. 650,000 seedlings. Roughly 4,000 women. Sixty-three self-help groups. Six sub-counties. Not one of those figures tells you whether a single tree survived, whether any coffee was sold, or whether one shilling reached a woman who did not previously control one.

That is the gap this film closes, and it is why the film has to be built as an inquiry rather than a commercial. If it is shot as a campaign video it will be dismissed as a campaign video, which is exactly what an opponent needs it to be. If it is shot as an honest account — including the trees that died — it becomes something none of her competitors can produce, because none of them has a three-year programme to account for.

The argument in one image

A coffee payment slip. A woman's name printed on it. Her hands holding it.

That is the whole film. Everything before it exists to explain why that piece of paper is unusual, and everything after it exists to say what it cost to get there. The programme's real innovation is not the seedlings — anyone can hand out seedlings. It is the charter mandating joint registration of coffee bushes between spouses, which is a property-rights reform delivered through an agricultural programme. Almost nobody in Nandi knows it exists.

What the film must answer, in order

  1. Why did Nandi stop growing coffee?
  2. Who did the work on the farms, and who collected the money?
  3. What did this programme actually do, mechanically?
  4. Three years on, what grew, what died, and who got paid?
  5. What does the person who built it say it was for?

Question five is last and it is short. A film that answers one through four honestly makes the case for her without ever having to argue it.

Kill criteria — read before spending anything

This film is only worth making if the outcomes are real. Before principal photography, establish seedling survival rate and whether beneficiaries have sold cherry. If survival is poor or income change is negligible, do not make this film. Report the finding privately to the campaign and pivot to Phase 3 delivery coverage instead.

Releasing a triumphant film about a programme that did not work is worse than releasing nothing. It hands the "political project" attack its best evidence, and it is done in public.

02

Structure

Three acts. The politician does not appear until minute seven, appears observationally in the middle, and speaks directly only in the last ninety seconds. That ratio is not modesty — it is the mechanism by which the film reads as evidence rather than advertising.

I0:00 – 7:00

The Lost Crop

Nandi is tea country. It was not always. Coffee was uprooted, cooperatives collapsed, pulping stations rusted. And on the farms that remained, a pattern: women did the weeding, the picking, the drying — and a man collected the payment. Establish the structural problem before anyone offers a solution.

II7:00 – 15:00

The Intervention

Not a politician arriving with a lorry. A mechanism: a nursery at Baraka, certified Ruiru 11 and Batian seedlings, agronomists, a self-help group of fifteen women meeting under a tree — and the charter. The signing is the centre of the film. This act shows a system being built, not a gift being given.

III15:00 – 24:00

The Ledger

Three years on. Cherry turning red on trees planted in 2023. Picking, weighing, the docket, the payment. Then the honest column: what failed, who dropped out, what the survival rate really was. Close with ninety seconds of the person who built it, and nothing more.

03

Cast

Four farmers carry the film. Cast them in September, before the harvest, so you have a relationship before you have a camera pointed at anyone. These are casting briefs, not people — find the real ones on the ground.

Casting rule: the woman must be able to tell you what changed in her own words, unprompted, in a pre-interview with no camera present. If she can only say the programme is good, she is not your subject.

Lead · Nandi Hills

The pilot-year farmer

Planted in 2023. Her trees are bearing now. She is the film's spine and the only one who can show a three-year arc on camera. Nandi Hills has over a thousand beneficiaries — the deepest casting pool — and it is where the August backlash originated, which makes an authentic voice from there worth more than one from anywhere else.

Second · Aldai

The Phase 2 farmer

Received seedlings at the Maraba launch in June 2025. Her trees are young. She provides the middle of the timeline and lets the film show the same story at two stages at once, which is what makes three years legible in twenty-four minutes.

Third · Mosop or Tinderet

The group chairwoman

Runs one of the sixty-three self-help groups. She talks about the collective, the training, the record-keeping, the arguments. She is the evidence that this is an organisation and not a mailing list — the single most important open question about the programme.

Anchor · Anywhere

The one it did not work for

Trees died, or she left the group, or the joint registration was refused at home. Non-negotiable. She is the reason a sceptical viewer believes the other three. Approach her with real care: she is being asked to say something unflattering about a programme her neighbours value, and about a candidate.

Supporting voices

  • An agronomist — Baraka Agricultural College or the county extension service. Technical spine. Says the words survival rate and yield on camera so the film does not have to assert them.
  • A cooperative official — someone who runs the scale and the ledger. Independent corroboration of who is being paid.
  • An elder or a husband — one man who supported joint registration and can say why. Crucial: without a male voice the film reads as a case against Nandi men, which is precisely the attack currently running.
  • Hon. Cynthia Muge — one sit-down, ninety seconds in the cut, plus observational coverage at distribution and the charter signing.
04

Visual grammar

Set the rules now and hold them. A consistent grammar is what separates a documentary from assembled footage, and it is also what will make the vertical cuts feel like they came from somewhere.

The four rules

  1. Hands before faces. This is a film about labour and ownership. Open on hands almost every time — picking, weighing, signing, counting. Cut to the face on the line that matters.
  2. The camera sits down. Interviews at the subject's eye level, seated, on her own ground — her farm, her doorway, never a hotel or an office. No standing-over angles on farmers, ever.
  3. Locked off for testimony, handheld for process. When someone is telling you something, the frame is still and the viewer trusts it. When something is happening, move with it.
  4. Available light. Golden hour at both ends of the day, negative fill rather than a key light. Coffee is grown in highland mist — shoot the mist rather than fighting it.

Palette to shoot for

The subject supplies it: red murram roads, wet dark-green leaf, the red of ripe cherry, the aluminium grey of the weighing scale, the white of a paper docket. Expose for the cherry and let the greens go deep. Grade cool in the mist sequences and warm only at payment — the film should feel like it changes temperature when the money arrives.

Two things to avoid

  • Drone as decoration. One aerial earns its place: the tea-to-coffee transition in Act I, showing the monoculture. Any other drone shot is a music video.
  • Slow motion on people's faces. It converts a person into a symbol. Reserve any speed ramp for cherry falling into a bucket, and use it once.

On shot 15.4

The end cards are the single most valuable forty seconds in the film and the reason it beats a press release. Sourcing each figure on screen — Nandi Coffee Cooperative records, November 2026 — is what converts the film from advocacy into a citable document. It is also the direct answer to the round unsourced numbers currently sitting on her website.

06

Sound design

The film has a sound argument as well as a visual one: it moves from the sound of work to the sound of paper. Labour is loud, physical, wet. Ownership is quiet, dry, precise. Build the whole design on that contrast and the payment scene will land without a single music cue.

Capture priorities

  • Lav on every interview, boom as safety. Rural highland means wind and livestock. Never rely on a single source you cannot replace.
  • Record wild sound obsessively. Stem snap, cherry into bucket, the scale settling, pen on carbon paper, the nursery irrigation. These are the film's vocabulary and none of them can be replaced by library effects convincingly.
  • Five minutes of room tone at every location. Non-negotiable, and it is the thing crews skip.
  • Interviews in Nandi. She and the farmers are most themselves in it, and the subtitles let the film travel to national press and diaspora at the same time.

Score

Sparse and late. First entry at the drone shot in Scene 3, out at the act break, back at Scene 10, resolved at 15.3. No score under Scene 13. The payment must be silent — scoring it tells the audience how to feel about a fact that speaks for itself, and it is the one place where a music cue would read as manipulation.

Source locally if at all possible: a Kalenjin instrument or voice, recorded for this film, is worth more than anything from a library and gives you a signature you can carry into every vertical cut.

Audio branding

Take the cherry-into-bucket impact from 1.5 and make it the campaign's audio mark. Two frames, distinctive, and it will appear at the head of every short. It costs nothing and it will make eight seconds of vertical video recognisable as hers before a word is spoken — which is the thing her channels currently lack entirely.

07

Ethics and consent

Read this before the first day of filming

This film asks rural women to discuss household money on camera, in a village where their husbands and neighbours will watch the result. That is a real risk to real people, and it is not covered by a standard release form. If a subject is harmed because of this film, the film has failed regardless of how it performs.

Protocol

  1. Explain the distribution honestly, in Nandi, before filming. This will be on Facebook and TikTok. Thousands of people locally will see it. Her husband will see it. Her chief will see it. Say all of that out loud.
  2. Written consent in the subject's own language, read aloud if literacy is a barrier, with a witness. Keep a copy with the subject, not only with you.
  3. Consent to be filmed is not consent to be in the final cut. Offer every subject a viewing of her own sequence before release, and honour a withdrawal without argument.
  4. Never film a woman describing a husband's control of income without her explicit, separately-obtained agreement on that specific topic. If there is any hesitation, use the group chairwoman speaking generally instead.
  5. Minors require guardian consent in writing, and default to not identifying them.
  6. The anchor farmer gets extra protection. She is criticising a programme her community values during a campaign. Offer anonymity — voice and hands only — as a genuine option, not a fallback.
  7. No inducement. Pay transport and time at a fair, documented rate. Never condition anything on what someone says.

The political line

You will be filming during a campaign, and the campaign will want the film to be more flattering than the evidence supports. Agree in writing, now, that Scene 14 is in the cut and the end-card figures are the measured ones. Get that agreed before you shoot, when it is cheap, rather than in January when it is expensive.

If that agreement cannot be obtained, this is a promotional video and should be scoped, budgeted and described as one — which is a legitimate thing to make, but it is not this document.

08

Schedule

Confirm this week

The exact local harvest window. The entire schedule hangs on it and the answer varies by region and altitude. Call Baraka Agricultural College or the county AFA office today. If the main crop in these sub-counties starts in October, prep time is roughly five weeks and casting must begin immediately.

Now
Verify before committing. Harvest dates. Seedling survival rate from AFA or county extension records. Whether any beneficiary has actually sold cherry. This is the kill-criteria check — do not skip it to get moving.
Sept
Recce and cast. Nandi Hills first. Meet twenty women, cast four. No camera on the first visit. Collect any 2023 photographs of planted plots for the before-and-after. Secure the nursery, cooperative and Baraka access. Locate the abandoned pulping station and the derelict society building.
Sept
Baseline data collection runs in parallel with casting. Survival counts, cooperative membership figures before and after, delivery volumes. The film's end cards depend on this and it is research work, not production work.
Oct–Dec
Principal photography. Harvest is the immovable window. Scenes 10 through 13 first — they cannot be rescheduled. Then Scenes 1 to 4 in the same period while the light and the mist are right. Shoot vertical variants of everything as you go.
Nov
Phase 3 distribution coverage whenever it happens — Scene 7 is event-dependent. Nursery, charter signing and group meeting scheduled around it.
Dec
Interviews with Muge (Scenes 5, 14, 15) and partner institutions. Deliberately late, so you can ask her about findings rather than intentions — including the failures.
Dec–Jan
Edit. Assembly, then the honesty pass: strip any shot with no evidentiary purpose. Subtitles, grade, mix. Source every end-card figure.
Mid-Jan
Release, ahead of the UDA nomination window. Screening tour of the six sub-counties first — village halls and churches before the internet. The shorts have been running continuously since October and have already built the audience for it.

Do not sit on the footage

Four months of material held back for one January release wastes the campaign's most valuable asset during its most important stretch. Cut and publish a vertical short every week from October. The film is the destination; the shorts are the road, and each one is also a live test of what her audience responds to.

With D1

Kit, for one or two people in rural Nandi

  • Camera: one body you know completely, one backup. Log profile, and shoot 4K so the vertical crop is a reframe rather than a compromise.
  • Glass: a fast prime for interviews, a mid zoom for process, a macro for the documents and the cherry. The macro matters more than it sounds — Scenes 8, 12 and 13 are all built on legible paper.
  • Audio: two lavs, a shotgun with proper wind protection, a recorder with timecode. Highland wind will ruin a shoot that skimps here.
  • Support: tripod for every locked-off interview. Handheld only for process, deliberately.
  • Light: a reflector and a black flag. One small LED for the document close-ups, and nothing else.
  • Power and weather: more batteries than you think, a power bank, a rain cover, silica for the humidity. Assume no mains at most locations.
  • Data: two copies before leaving any location. No exceptions, ever, especially on harvest days you cannot repeat.
